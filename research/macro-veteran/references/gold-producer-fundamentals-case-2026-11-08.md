## What the filings say about earnings conversion

### 1. A higher realized gold price does not flow one-for-one into free cash flow

For Agnico, Q3 realized price increased about 23% year over year while payable production fell about 6%. Total cash cost and AISC per ounce also rose. Its reported free cash flow declined from $1.190bn to $852m, yet free cash flow before non-cash working-capital changes was $1.057bn versus $1.035bn. The gap is material: Q3 cash flow included a seasonal increase in supplies inventory ahead of the Nunavut sealift, while capital additions rose to $903m from $626m. The earnings story is positive on price realization but mixed on output, costs and cash timing.

Newmont reported $2.768bn operating cash flow and $1.912bn free cash flow after $565m of net working-capital outflow. The company's realized-price bridge includes a $26/oz mark-to-market benefit from provisional pricing, so the reported $4,317/oz is not just the average spot price received on physical settlement. Newmont's Q4 guidance also points to elevated unit costs: it expects $1,900/oz AISC for the quarter versus $1,454/oz in Q3, alongside $600m sustaining and $520m development capital. This is a forecast, not an observed outcome.

Barrick's latest quarter shows why consolidated and shareholder-attributable cash figures must be separated. Q2 consolidated free cash flow was $515m, but the attributable figure was $141m after equity-investee and non-controlling-interest adjustments. Q2 repurchases of $1.209bn plus $288m of dividends exceeded that attributable FCF. This does not by itself prove an unsustainable distribution policy; it does mean that cash returns in that quarter were not funded solely from attributable FCF.

### 2. “AISC” is not a fully comparable accounting line

The World Gold Council's guidance describes AISC as a non-GAAP cost construct incorporating items such as mine operating costs, royalties, corporate overhead, reclamation, expensed exploration and sustaining capital. Companies still make judgments about sustaining versus project capital and differ in numerator treatment, ounces basis, by-product credits, joint-venture attribution and special items. The WGC itself notes that the measure requires transparent reconciliation. [WGC AISC guidance and FAQs](https://www.gold.org/about-gold/gold-supply/responsible-gold/all-in-costs)

Newmont's headline by-product AISC is based on gold ounces sold, while Agnico's figure is calculated on its disclosed production-ounce basis; Agnico follows the WGC guidance except for its stated treatment of Nunavut Tunngavik Inc. payments. Barrick's per-ounce measure uses attributable gold sales and includes its proportionate share of equity-method investee costs. Even the Q3 values are therefore not a clean cost ranking. Do not compare Newmont's $1,454/oz directly with Agnico's $1,648/oz or Barrick's Q2 $1,866/oz as if they shared a numerator, denominator, reporting period and portfolio perimeter. Prefer each issuer's reconciliation to IFRS/GAAP costs and compare cost changes within the same issuer over matched periods.

### 3. Balance-sheet strength has timing and availability conditions

Newmont ended September with $8.827bn cash and $12.827bn total liquidity, but it had announced a $1.95bn cash payment to Barrick in Q4 under their amended Nevada Gold Mines agreement. The September net-cash figure predates that use of cash. Its company-defined net cash also deducts $497m of lease and other financing obligations from the $3.740bn cash-over-debt position. Newmont disclosed that $2.201bn of cash held abroad may incur withholding taxes if repatriated and that some Argentine peso cash is restricted. Gross cash is not all immediately fungible parent-company liquidity.

Agnico had $3.613bn cash against $197m debt and an undrawn revolving facility. The facility was increased after quarter-end from $2bn to $3bn, with a 2031 maturity; distinguish the quarter-end balance sheet from the subsequent amendment. Its production outlook also carries a specific operating risk: the July Barnat rock-mass movement led management to expect output near the low end of 3.3–3.5 Moz.

Barrick had $5.927bn cash and $4.682bn debt at June 30, with $1.245bn net cash and a $3bn undrawn credit facility. Its debt schedule shows no principal due through 2030 and $4.630bn in 2031 and thereafter; lease obligations are separate. The net-cash position fell during Q2 alongside substantial repurchases, so the quarter-end stock cannot be read without its financing flows.

## Explicit gold-price exposure and hedge asymmetry

- **Newmont:** its SEC filing says it held no instruments designated to hedge metal-price risk at September 30. That statement does not eliminate exposure from provisional concentrate pricing; the Q3 release's realized-price bridge shows a $26/oz mark-to-market contribution. It separately uses currency and power-related hedges.
- **Barrick:** 650,000 oz remained in zero-cost collars at June 30, with purchased puts at $3,100/oz and sold calls at $4,310/oz, scheduled monthly through August 2028. The put supplies a floor for covered ounces; the short calls give up some upside above the cap. In Q2 these instruments caused an $18m realized loss recorded in revenue, and the issuer attributed part of its realized-price discount to the collars.
- **Agnico Eagle:** the Q3 release reports a $12.5m derivative loss and notes diesel and foreign-exchange hedges, but does not provide a gold-sales hedge-book schedule. Mark gold-price hedge exposure **not disclosed in this release**, not zero.

Do not infer each company's gold beta from the realized-price line alone. Sale timing, pricing lags, concentrate terms, provisionals, collars, by-product credits, royalties, production mix, FX, fuel, taxes, working capital and capital spending all intervene between gold prices and shareholder cash flow.

2. **Reconcile production to sales:** preserve payable, attributable, consolidated and sold-ounce definitions. Track concentrate inventory and provisional-pricing balances where reported.
3. **Bridge operating cash to FCF:** use the company's own reconciliation; separately show working capital, taxes, sustaining capex, growth/project capex, capitalized interest and minority/equity-investee adjustments.
4. **Track full-year guide versus actual:** record original and revised production, cost and capex guidance, then compare the eventual result with the forecast vintage that was public at the time.
5. **Check hedge terms and maturities:** convert ounces covered to a share of expected production only when periods match; retain strike, tenor, volume, premium/collar structure, mark-to-market and realized effects.
6. **Map operational risks to affected output:** mine incidents, grade/recovery, water/energy, permitting, jurisdiction, labor, tailings and planned shutdowns. Avoid turning a group-level production change into a single-cause claim.
