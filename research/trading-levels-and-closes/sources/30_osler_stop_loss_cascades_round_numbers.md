## Reported content
> "Both stop-loss orders and take-profit orders tend to cluster at round numbers, with almost 10 percent of all such orders placed at rates ending in 00 (such as ¥123.00/$ or $1.4300/£)." (paraphrased from search snippet)
> "Executed stop-loss buy orders cluster most strongly just above round numbers, and executed stop-loss sell orders cluster most strongly just below round numbers." (paraphrased)
> "Exchange rates tend to move rapidly after reaching levels where stop-loss orders cluster, indicating that a trend can be prolonged by the execution of some stop-loss orders triggered by that trend." (paraphrased)
> Data source: order book of the Royal Bank of Scotland, Aug 1, 1999–Apr 11, 2000, 9,655 orders, aggregate face value >$55 billion, across USD/JPY, GBP/USD, EUR/USD.

## Relevance
This is the single strongest, most-cited academic basis for round numbers behaving differently than other price points — not because of any inherent "support/resistance" property but because of a **documented mechanical fact**: real institutional stop-loss and take-profit orders cluster densely just around round numbers (round-number clustering of ~10% of orders vs. a flat/random distribution would predict far less). Osler's causal story is specific and important to preserve: take-profit orders cluster on the *approach* to round numbers (creating a brake/reversal effect), while stop-loss orders cluster and trigger *just after* the round number is crossed (creating an acceleration/cascade effect, i.e., breakout continuation). This is not folklore — it's an order-book-level empirical finding from a real bank's proprietary order flow, which is the highest-quality evidence type available for microstructure claims. It directly and mechanically explains why round numbers can act as both "reversal" zones (pre-level) and "breakout accelerant" zones (post-level breach) in the same instrument, which resolves an apparent contradiction in retail folklore.
