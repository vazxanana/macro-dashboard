## Reported content
> "Published support and resistance levels predict intraday trend interruptions significantly more often than arbitrary levels, achieving a 60.8% bounce frequency compared to 56.2% for random levels." (paraphrased from search snippet)
> "The predictive power of support and resistance levels lasts at least five business days post-publication, with statistically significant bounce frequencies in nine of the sixteen firm-currency cases." (paraphrased)
> Methodology: bootstrap technique comparing actual bounce frequencies of published S/R levels (from major bank technical-analysis desks) against 10,000 sets of randomly generated levels, using intraday German mark, Japanese yen, and British pound vs. USD data.
> Bounce-frequency edge over random levels: mark +4.2 pp, yen +5.6 pp, pound +4.0 pp (paraphrased).

## Relevance
This is the second pillar of Osler's work and is distinct from the round-number/stop-cascade paper: it tests **specific levels published by real bank technical-analysis desks** (not round numbers per se) against a rigorous random-level null (bootstrap with 10,000 draws), and finds a modest but statistically significant edge (~4-8 bounce frequency vs. random, roughly 60% vs 56%). This is a genuinely strong, peer-reviewed-adjacent result for the concept of "support/resistance" as a real (if small) statistical phenomenon in FX — not zero, not huge. It's the best available direct rebuttal to the "S/R is pure random-walk noise" objection, precisely because it uses randomized levels as the control group rather than eyeballing charts. Caveat: effect size is modest (a ~4-6 point edge), evidence is FX-specific (DEM, JPY, GBP vs USD), and levels tested were desk-published levels, not any arbitrary line a retail trader draws.
