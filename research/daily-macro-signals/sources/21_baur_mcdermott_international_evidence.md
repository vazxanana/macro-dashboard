## Reported quotes/paraphrases (not independently verified against primary-source text)
> Covering 1979-2009: "gold is both a hedge and a safe haven for major European stock markets and the US but not for Australia, Canada, Japan and large emerging markets such as the BRIC countries."

> "Gold was a strong safe haven for most developed markets during the peak of the recent financial crisis."

> The paper "distinguish[es] between weak and strong forms of the safe haven" — per the terminology, if the correlation between gold and the stressed asset during a crisis is exactly zero (rather than negative), gold is classified as only a "weak" safe haven for that market.

> Gold is described as "the stronger and more immediate safe haven" specifically around the 9/11 attacks and the global financial crisis, implying an immediate (i.e., same-window) rather than delayed reaction.

## Relevance
This paper reinforces that gold's safe-haven property is (a) geographically conditional — it holds for the US/major European markets but explicitly fails for Australia, Canada, Japan, and BRIC equities — and (b) graded in strength (strong vs. weak safe haven, based on whether the crisis-period correlation is negative or merely zero), not a uniform, always-on property. Combined with source 20's "short-lived" finding, this supports treating any VIX/credit-spread-driven "gold should rise" signal as conditional on (i) which market is in crisis, (ii) how severe/extreme the stress is, and (iii) a very narrow contemporaneous time window — not as a stable daily directional edge that persists into the next session.
