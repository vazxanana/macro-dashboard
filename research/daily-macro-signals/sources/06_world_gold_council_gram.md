## Reported quotes/paraphrases (not independently verified against primary-source text)
> GRAM is "a multiple regression model of monthly gold price returns," which groups gold's performance into four thematic driver categories: economic expansion, risk & uncertainty, opportunity cost, and momentum.

> "These themes capture motives behind gold demand, most importantly investment demand, which is considered the marginal driver of gold price returns in the short run."

> On a recent period: "reduced opportunity cost through weaker US dollar and marginally lower rates contributed 10 percentage points to gold's year-to-date return," while "the high-risk environment explained roughly 12 percentage points of gold's year-to-date return, primarily driven by geopolitical risk."

> General statement: "historically, rate hikes have tended to be negative for gold if they strengthen the US dollar, lift real yields and boost sentiment" — a conditional/contemporaneous statement, not an unconditional daily rule.

## Relevance
GRAM is probably the single most-cited "real rates are a gold driver" framework in industry practice, and it is explicit that its unit of analysis is **monthly** gold returns decomposed via contemporaneous regression against monthly opportunity-cost (real rate/dollar), risk, and momentum factors. It is a decomposition/attribution model (explaining realized monthly returns after the fact), not a predictive model forecasting future returns from today's real-rate level. This is a useful, high-visibility example of exactly the horizon mismatch the research question is probing: an industry-standard "real rates drive gold" framework that operates at monthly frequency and in an explanatory (contemporaneous), not predictive, mode — yet gets casually cited as if it justified daily directional calls.
